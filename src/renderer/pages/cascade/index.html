The computer WebTV Internet Terminal

Not every late-90s web surfer owned a beige tower. In July 1996 a Silicon Valley start-up called WebTV Networks rolled out “a low-cost device to browse the Internet’s World Wide Web” [source 6]. It needed just a phone line, a TV and a universal remote [source 6].

Sony and Philips built the first boxes, and they went on sale that September [source 6]. Inside hummed a 33,600 bits-per-second modem, “slightly faster than the current standard in PCs” [source 6]. The logic board was designed “to allow home users to access the internet using their television set as a display device,” and it came with a built-in modem and an infrared keyboard [source 7].

Today the Computer History Museum keeps both a WebTV logic board and a Sony “Internet Terminal” in its collection [source 7][source 8]. The Sony unit’s front panel simply says “webtv” [source 8]. So grab the remote, sink into the sofa and let the late-90s web come to you in glorious living-room vision.

The person Håkon Wium Lie

Håkon Wium Lie gave the web its sense of style. On 10 October 1994, writing from a CERN e-mail address, he published “Cascading HTML style sheets -- a proposal” [source 9]. The proposal introduced the idea of an ordered list, or “cascade”, of style sheets [source 9]. He worked on the World Wide Web project with Tim Berners-Lee and Robert Cailliau [source 10].

Bert Bos was among the first people to respond to the draft, and the two joined forces [source 11]. Their work became a W3C Recommendation as CSS level 1 in December 1996, and CSS level 2 followed in May 1998 [source 11]. In 1999 he joined Opera Software “to ensure that the specifications were implemented correctly by at least one browser” [source 12].

He served as its CTO from 1999 until 2016 [source 10]. His University of Oslo PhD thesis, submitted in 2005, has a fittingly simple title: “Cascading Style Sheets” [source 12]. Every colour, font and margin on this page owes him a small thank-you.
