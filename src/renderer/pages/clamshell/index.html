The computer The clamshell iBook

Pop the lid on pure candy. Apple introduced the iBook at the Macworld Expo keynote in New York on July 21, 1999, in two “eye-catching colors”: Blueberry and Tangerine [source 13]. Its rubber-coated translucent case came with a built-in handle [source 14], which made it a laptop you could swing like a lunchbox. Inside sat a PowerPC G3 processor and a 12.1″ display, with “all day” battery life of up to 6 hours [source 13].

The real magic was invisible. Apple billed it as “the first consumer portable to provide untethered Internet access for home and schools via AirPort”, its new wireless local area network [source 13]. AirPort moved data at up to 11 Mbps over a range of about 150 feet [source 14].

Prices began around $1,600 when it arrived that September [source 14]. Surfing the web with no cable at all? In 1999 that felt like science fiction wrapped in a tangerine shell.

The person Jeffrey Zeldman

Jeffrey Zeldman helped persuade the web to play by the rules. In 1998 he co-founded the Web Standards Project with Glenn Davis and George Olsen [source 15]. The project was a grassroots coalition that warned that ever-more incompatible browsers would make the cost of building websites skyrocket [source 16]. He led the group from 1999 to 2003 [source 15].

He is the founder and publisher of A List Apart [source 15], which began as a mailing list in 1997 and launched as a website in 1998 [source 17]. Its focus has always been “web standards and best practices” [source 17]. His book Designing With Web Standards first appeared in 2003 [source 15].

With Eric Meyer he co-founded the conference An Event Apart, which ran from December 2005 until late 2023 [source 15]. He is Founder Emeritus of the design studio Happy Cog, and his own site has been online since May 1995 [source 15]. Thanks to people like him, one page can look right in every browser.
