The computer NeXT Computer, “the Cube”

Say hello to the little black box where the web woke up. The NeXT Computer was nicknamed “The Cube” for its one-foot, die-cast magnesium case, and it ran the Unix-based NeXTSTEP operating system [source 2]. In September 1990, Tim Berners-Lee took delivery of one at CERN, and by December the Web was up and running, “albeit between just a couple of computers at CERN” [source 1].

A NeXT computer was used by Berners-Lee to devise the World Wide Web and acted as the world’s first web server [source 2]. His own machine wears just one decoration: a torn sticker that reads “This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!” [source 3]. That may be the most important sticky note in computing history.

The computer belongs to CERN, which lent it to London’s Science Museum for display in 2014 [source 3]. So the next time a page pops up in a blink, tip your hat to a magnesium cube that was politely asked never, ever to take a nap.

The person Sir Tim Berners-Lee

Sir Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989 while working at CERN, and in 1990 he wrote the first web client and server [source 4]. It began with a paper, “Information Management: A Proposal”, which he submitted to his boss, Mike Sendall [source 1]. CERN still keeps info.cern.ch online as the “home of the first website” [source 5].

In 1994 he founded the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), which develops the web’s open standards, and he is now its Founder and Emeritus Director [source 4]. He was knighted in 2004 and received the Order of Merit in 2007 [source 4]. In 2017 he received the ACM A.M. Turing Award “for inventing the World Wide Web” [source 4].

He is also a professor of computer science at Oxford and an emeritus professor at MIT [source 4]. Every link you click in RetroNet follows the path he first drew on a NeXT cube.
